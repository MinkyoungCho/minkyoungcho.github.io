Does a robot know what its body can do?

NavAny is a benchmark for physical self-models. The same scene and the same instruction go to different bodies, and only an agent that knows its own body picks the right action for each.

Minkyoung Cho. Project page, work in progress.

Why it matters

Generalist agents now drive humanoids, legged robots, wheeled manipulators and drones. A doorway can be passable for one body and not for another, and a body changes the moment it picks up a box. An agent that misjudges its body either attempts what it cannot do, which is unsafe, or refuses what it could do, which wastes the robot.

Existing evaluations either hand the body to the model as a text profile, or vary the body without changing the right answer. Neither can tell whether a model actually knows its body, and neither says how to make it better.

What we aim for

The benchmark is a means, not the end. We use it to find training signals that make self-models better.

  1. Diagnose

    Measure each self-model capability with matched pairs and boundary curves, separately for prediction and correction.

  2. Find proxy signals

    Keep only the diagnostics that predict performance on bodies the model has never seen, beyond plain success rate.

  3. Improve

    Turn them into rewards or auxiliary losses, train, and re-test on held-out bodies and mid-task body changes.

Design questions

Like memory in RoboMME, body knowledge raises three design questions. NavAny answers them with controlled comparisons on fixed backbones.

What does an agent need to know about its body?

Four capabilities, from fitting the body now to staying correct as it changes.

In what form should that knowledge be given?

Nothing, a learned embodiment token, a text spec, a numeric capability vector, or an interaction history with probing.

Where should it enter the model?

In the planner or the controller, as input or as feature-wise modulation.

The capability ladder

Four rungs, each with two navigation and two loco-manipulation tasks. A model that cannot fit its body now cannot stay feasible as it changes, so the first rung a model fails shows what it is missing.

How a task is scored

  • Matched pairs

    Same scene, same instruction, different body or body state, and a different correct answer. A body-blind policy must get at least one of the two wrong.

  • Two-sided scoring

    Attempting the impossible and refusing the possible both fail. The cautious option always costs the capable body something, so neither always-go nor always-cautious can pass a pair.

  • Boundary curves

    Sweep the body-scaled ratio, such as gap width over body width, and compare where the model judges the boundary with where it really is. This follows the psychophysics of affordances (Warren 1984; Warren and Whang 1987).

  • Use, infer, update

    Every task runs with body information given and withheld. C4 adds changes the agent is not told about.

Sixteen tasks

Closed-loop mockups on a floor plan with a 1 m grid. Every task replicates a classic paradigm and plays the same pair under three policies: self-model, always-default and always-cautious. Only the self-model policy passes. Select a mockup to open it at full size.

How NavAny differs

Navigation and loco-manipulation benchmarks either give the body to the model or vary it without changing the answer. NavAny makes body knowledge necessary and measures it.

Real-robot validation, planned

We will validate on a Unitree G1. Pairs come from changing the same robot's body state rather than swapping robots.

Ground-truth boundaries hold on the robot

Measure each boundary on the physical G1 and in simulation with the same controller, and check that the boundary shifts by the same amount when the body changes.
